Here is my poorly researched crackpot rant (math teachers, call me out):

While I've been learning about abstract algebra and related topics for a couple of years now. I have not done much teaching of the concepts. I hoped I could make an introduction that was both thorough and not too arcane. I started with what I thought was a "clean" motivating idea for the topic, but in developing the explanation, it quickly became much more equation dense and "abstract" than I hoped. Upon reflection of the texts I relied on to build my understanding I noticed a similar balance that must be struck between relying on the student's intuition about things they already know, and using precise definitions that are arcane and hard to approach.

But why can't either of these suffice to teach abstract algebra?

## Teaching with existing intuition

The problem with teaching discrete math and abstract algebra as part of a broad math curriculum, is that understanding groups, fields and other structures comes from a  **contraction** of unnecessary definitions, whereas the way we build students intuition about math -- all the way from elementary school -- is an **expansion** of what numbers are. Students start in elementary school with natural numbers and basic arithmetic. Natural numbers are *expanded* in to Integers and Rational numbers with fractions and then to Real numbers with concepts like pi and square roots. Their intuition towards numbers is further *expanded* with trigonometry and calculus. The problem with abstract algebra for students who were taught like this is that *their understanding of what a number is, is basically strictly real numbers*. And while its relatively easy for a student to accept that when it is pointing out to them, I don't think that means the student has let go of all the psychological baggage it comes with. It is like fish A saying "The water is nice today" and fish b responding "What's water?"

This "psychological baggage" is [the conflict between concept image and concept definition](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/507/1/WRAP_Tall_dot1981a-concept-image.pdf)

For example, a student encountering an equation like (a\*b+c)/d will bring their intuition about what are valid things you can do to the equation like "take its derivative" or "calculate the sin of the result". Actions that may not be defined within the scope of the structure a,b,c,d is in.

## Teaching with precise definitions

The issue with precise definitions are a bit more obvious. Namely that they are intimidating and easy to lose track of what means what as you go about developing them. The reason *why* working with precise definitions is intimidating needs to be examined more closely.

For the purposes of this post, the structure of a topic in mathematics is a [dag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_acyclic_graph) with axioms on the bottom and theorems based on those axioms filling out out the graph. Being able to show how theorems are derived from other theorems is approximately what we mean when we say that someone "understands" a topic in math. With this framework of "understanding" the most efficient way to get a student to understand math is to introduce those axioms and logically present all the consequent theorems. This is approximately what math education does and I would like to posit part of the reason students feel intimidated is how this structure fails to map to psychological concepts of "understanding" and doesn't account for the power of exploration and "being wrong". Math education develops students concept image of math in an inflexible way.

Part of this is game theoretic, students (and also teachers and schools) are incentivised to get good grades, and they optimize for their own laziness. What this means is they will look for any shortcut heuristic even if it hurts their long term understanding for a temporary gain.

## What is it that I want?

I guess I'm just wish-casting a world where students would explore math with more curiosity and willingness to be wrong, but this is a losing strategy in the adversarial system we have against our peers.
