Yesterday I devoured Victor Frankl's "Man's Search for Meaning" in a single evening.
The theme of it is, of course, "what man is capable of", both good and evil.

## Holocaust

The last time I had really consumed an account of the holocaust was in eighth grade with Elie Wiesel's "Night", so I think the time difference added 
to the weight of the book. That's not to say that we *should* ever forget/get used to the horrors of the holocaust, but part of the story of the victims survival was how they became apathetic/detached to what was going on around them. I don't do it justice with that explanation which is why I would recommend reading it yourself.

## Logotherapy

The quote from Nietzsche -- "he who has a why to live can bear almost any how" -- is repeated many times throughout the book. He  In general, I felt that Frankl puts himself at odds with absurdists like Camus in his rejection of nihilism (Camus would call it philosophical suicide I guess?). It has been a while since I've read Camus so I would like to come back to this idea.

The notion of "hyper-intention" and "paradoxical intention" reminded me of Mark Manson's "Backward's Law" ([Manson has recommends this book too](https://youtu.be/7kwqWgXzHvc?si=ZIoaQHu7sx87VzT-&t=425)).  They are sort sort of just inverses of each other. Whereas paradoxical intention is "the phobic patient intending precisely that which he fears"(Frankl) the backwards law is: ["desiring a positive experience is itself a negative experience; accepting a negative experience is a positive experience."](https://markmanson.net/the-backwards-law). There is a necessary distinction between intention and desire that doesn't make the mapping one-to-one, but I do think it rhymes.

Manson and Frankl also agree on the stupidity of "the pursuit of happiness." In this book Frankl argues happiness cannot be pursued but must ensue. Reflecting on Manson's work in light of reading Frankl, do think that a significant part of "The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F\*ck" is just logotherapy repackaged for Millenials. 

Of course the red blooded patriots among you may be wondering if by condemning "the pursuit of happiness" I am taking down the declaration of independence with me, but the real issue here is a relatively modern conception of happiness as a mood that you are in rather than what Jefferson intended as "eudaimonic" happiness, meaning human flourishing and fulfilling one's true potential. Admittedly that definition is less fun, but the declaration of independence was written with the intention of building a country "by the people" and so there was a vested interest in the people being virtuous, a fact that I fear is being forgotten today. I think America's obsession with happiness (understood as a mood to be in) is bad.
